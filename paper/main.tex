\begin{abstract}
Automatic prompt optimizers are marketed on aggregate accuracy and increasingly applied
to low-resource languages. On a subject-partitioned Uzbek university-entrance benchmark
we run the audit that aggregate accuracy prevents: with $5{,}058$ paired items from a
frozen corpus never used in development, \textsc{DSPy}'s \bsfs{} improves reasoning
subjects ($+2.3$, $p{=}0.029$) and does not improve knowledge subjects ($-1.2$,
$p{=}0.15$), leaving overall accuracy flat ($+0.1$, $p{=}0.92$). The reasoning gain and
the knowledge shortfall offset each other, so a practitioner reading one number sees
nothing at all. Tested directly rather than as two marginal tests, the reallocation is
significant across items (knowledge-versus-reasoning odds ratio $1.40$ $[1.08, 1.81]$)
but not across models, which do not share a common effect ($I^2{=}66\%$; treating models
as exchangeable gives $1.33$ $[0.83, 2.14]$).
The users' own language is simply a knowledge subject: it is not distinguishable from
the benchmark's other knowledge subject, which fares marginally worse ($-1.1$ vs.\
$-1.4$). We report this against our own initial finding, which the same data overturns.
At $n{\approx}100$ per model---the scale at which subject-level audits are normally
run---we measured a native-language-specific effect ($p{=}0.011$; odds ratio $2.27$
against non-native items, with the benchmark's other knowledge subject at a clean
$1.04$). Powered on held-out data it dissolves: the odds ratio falls to $1.25$
$[0.98, 1.61]$ and the other knowledge subject rises to $1.16$, statistically
indistinguishable. A second correction follows the same pattern: a one-line
brevity-constraint repair we proposed turns out to be inert ($p{=}0.90$). Substituting
different correct demonstrations does help ($p{=}0.0097$), which would indict
correctness-based selection itself---but we show that a fixed shuffle seed makes six
models select only eight distinct demonstrations between them, so that contrast rests on
one \bsfs{} draw and we report it as an observation rather than a finding. We release the
harness, per-item logs, and a one-command verifier for every number.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Chain-of-thought prompting~\citep{wei2022cot} helps mainly on math and symbolic
reasoning~\citep{sprague2025cot} and can hurt elsewhere~\citep{liu2025mind}. Layered on
top, automatic prompt optimizers---most prominently \textsc{DSPy}~\citep{khattab2024dspy}
with its \bsfs{} teleprompter, which retains as few-shot demonstrations only training
examples the model itself answers correctly---are sold as turnkey accuracy boosters and
increasingly applied to low-resource languages.

They are also evaluated the way they are sold: on one aggregate number. This paper asks
what that number conceals, on a benchmark whose subject partition makes the question
answerable, and at a sample size that makes the answer stable.

\paragraph{The finding.} \bsfs{} does not raise accuracy uniformly. It raises
\emph{reasoning}-subject accuracy and leaves \emph{knowledge}-subject accuracy where it
was, and on our benchmark the two move the aggregate by a tenth of a point between them
(\S\ref{sec:powered}). Aggregate accuracy is not merely a coarse summary here; it is
blind by construction. Any deployment whose users care disproportionately about the
knowledge half---which, for a national-language application, is the whole point---is
paying for a gain it does not receive.

\paragraph{The correction.} Our own first pass through this data reached a sharper and
more alarming conclusion: that the harm was specific to the \emph{native language}. At
$n{\approx}100$ items per model, that finding looked solid---a significant pooled
effect, a $2.27$ odds ratio against non-native items, and a placebo rotation in which
the benchmark's other knowledge subject sat at $1.04$. Re-run on a frozen corpus at four
times the scale, it dissolves (\S\ref{sec:powered}). The native subject and the other
knowledge subject become indistinguishable. We report both, because the gap between them
is the most useful thing we found: subject-level audits at the scale they are normally
run at can manufacture a language-specific story out of a subject-type effect.

\paragraph{A second correction, same shape.} We proposed a one-line repair---require
bootstrapped demonstrations to satisfy the prompt's own brevity instruction---and
measured it recovering most of the loss. An ablation separating the two things that
repair changes at once shows the constraint itself is inert ($p{=}0.90$); what helps is
substituting different demonstrations. That would indict correctness-based selection,
except that the optimizer's selection turns out to be near-degenerate: a fixed pool
ordering leads six models to choose only eight distinct demonstrations between them, so
the contrast compares one \bsfs{} draw against several random ones (\S\ref{sec:demos}).
We report it as an observation, name the experiment that would settle it, and do not
build on it.

\paragraph{What survives unchanged.} A format route from optimizer to failure is real
and causally verified: correctness-selection imports verbose demonstrations, a fixed
decoding budget truncates them, and raising the budget improves accuracy in exactly the
models that truncate and not in a zero-truncation control (\S\ref{sec:budget}). It
accounts for $17.6\%$ of harmful flips at scale---a genuine and repairable route, and a
minority of the effect.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{CoT and prompt optimization.} CoT's gains concentrate on math and symbolic
tasks~\citep{sprague2025cot}; it can reduce accuracy where deliberation hurts
humans~\citep{liu2025mind}, and its marginal value on strong models is
shrinking~\citep{meincke2025decreasing}. \textsc{DSPy}~\citep{khattab2024dspy} and
\textsc{MIPROv2}~\citep{opsahlong2024mipro} self-bootstrap demonstrations.
\citet{sarmah2024} conjectured that teleprompters over-rely on examples the model already
answers well. We find that skew is an artifact of comparing against the wrong baseline
(\S\ref{sec:budget}): what the selected demonstrations share is verbosity, not subject.
\paragraph{Format restrictions and budgets.} Format constraints degrade reasoning
performance~\citep{tam2024format}, and tokenizers price languages
unequally~\citep{petrov2023tokenizer} with accuracy consequences across low-resource
languages~\citep{lundin2025tokentax}. We connect this to prompt optimization: the
optimizer \emph{generates} format pressure, which fixed budgets convert into
failures---though through input length and induced verbosity rather than output-side
fertility (\S\ref{sec:regimes}).
\paragraph{Cross-lingual reasoning dynamics.} A parallel literature asks which language
multilingual models reason \emph{in}~\citep{wendler2024llamas,schut2025english,
zhong2024english}, and how language-centric pretraining shapes
reasoning~\citep{park2025collapse,tran2025pivoted}. Our powered result reduces the pull
of these accounts for this phenomenon: what we observe separates subject \emph{types},
not languages.
\paragraph{Disparate impact and evaluation scale.} \citet{zhu2022rich} show
semi-supervised learning can help high-baseline subgroups at low-baseline subgroups'
expense, masked by averages. Our result is the prompt-optimization analogue, with the
subgroup axis turning out to be subject type. Native Turkic benchmarks include
TUMLU~\citep{tumlu2025}, KazMMLU~\citep{kazmmlu2025} and
TurkishMMLU~\citep{turkishmmlu2024}; DTM~\citep{dtm2026} provides the subject partition
our analyses require.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data.} DTM~\citep{dtm2026}: $1{,}000$ four-option Uzbek questions
(\ona{}, Uzbek grammar and literature, $400$; \tarix{}, history, $300$; mathematics
$150$; physics $150$). Seeded stratified split (train $500$/dev $249$/test $251$).
Eight rows of the release file carry nulled answers: two fall in the test split and were
recovered from run logs, six are excluded. \textbf{Replication corpus:} the DTM~2019
public release contributes $2{,}028$ deduplicated items across the same four subjects
($393$ \ona{}, $504$ \tarix{}, $727$ mathematics, $404$ physics), never used in any
development decision, with its protocol frozen before first model contact. This is the
evaluation set for \S\ref{sec:powered}.
\paragraph{Models and engines.} Six open instruction-tuned LLMs spanning
\textsc{gemma-4} (\textsc{e4b}, \textsc{31b}) and \textsc{qwen-3.5/3.6} ($4$--$27$B),
served with Ollama at 4-bit (Q4\_K\_M) on an NVIDIA DGX Spark, plus vLLM at bf16 for the
three large models and an Apple-silicon replication. Greedy decoding,
\texttt{max\_tokens} $512$ unless manipulated.
\paragraph{Conditions.} \textbf{cot} (brevity-constrained zero-shot CoT: ``at most two
sentences''), \textbf{bootstrap} (\bsfs{} on the CoT module, $4$ demonstrations,
correctness metric), \textbf{bootstrap-compliant} (the metric additionally requires the
demonstration to satisfy the brevity instruction), and \textbf{random-$k$} (four
demonstrations drawn uniformly from the model's own correct traces, \S\ref{sec:demos}).
\paragraph{Instrumentation.} Every generation logs raw completion text, token counts,
finish reason and a parse-failure flag. We score two ways: \emph{deployment} (a parse
failure is wrong---what a user experiences) and \emph{knowledge} (items where either
condition truncated or failed to parse are excluded).
\paragraph{Statistics.} Paired exact binomial McNemar throughout, Holm-corrected within
each contrast family. Subject contrasts use a Mantel--Haenszel odds ratio stratified by
model, with intervals from a bootstrap resampling \emph{items} as whole clusters, since
all models answer the same items. Every number regenerates from committed per-item logs
by a single command in the released harness.

We evaluated the demonstrations \bsfs{} selects---the identical compiled programs, not a
fresh compile---against the frozen four-subject replication corpus, for all six models
(Table~\ref{tab:powered}). Three things follow.

\paragraph{The optimizer works, on half the benchmark.} Reasoning subjects gain $+2.3$
points ($b{=}155$, $c{=}197$, $n{=}1800$, $p{=}0.029$). That is the effect \bsfs{} is
sold on and it is real.

\paragraph{Knowledge subjects do not benefit.} Pooled, they move $-1.2$ ($b{=}349$,
$c{=}311$, $p{=}0.150$)---directionally negative, not significant. We state the
conservative form: the optimizer does not \emph{help} knowledge subjects. Whether it
mildly harms them is beyond this design's resolution, and we do not claim it.

\paragraph{The aggregate is blind.} Overall accuracy moves $+0.1$ points
($b{=}504$, $c{=}508$, $p{=}0.925$). The reasoning gain and the knowledge shortfall
offset each other. A practitioner who reads the benchmark score sees a null result
and concludes the optimizer did nothing; in fact it moved accuracy between subjects at a
rate their aggregate cannot express. This is the paper's practical point, and it does
not depend on any language-specific claim.

\paragraph{The reallocation, tested directly.} A significant reasoning gain reported
beside a non-significant knowledge null is two marginal tests, and the difference between
them is not itself one. We therefore ran the differential estimator used throughout this
paper with the label grouped by subject \emph{type}: conditional on an item changing
correctness, are the odds it changed for the worse higher for knowledge items than for
reasoning items? Pooled over six models it gives $1.40$ $[1.08, 1.81]$ (bootstrap
$P(\mathrm{OR}{\le}1){=}0.008$; Table~\ref{tab:placebo}, last row). That is this paper's
central claim as a single estimate, and unlike the native-versus-rest contrast it
survives powering: the same estimator falls from $2.67$ to $1.40$ and still excludes $1$,
where the native contrast falls from $2.27$ to $1.25$ and no longer does. The
reallocation is the finding; the language was the coincidence.

\paragraph{But the models do not agree.} Mantel--Haenszel assumes the strata share one
odds ratio, and Cochran's $Q$ rejects that ($Q{=}14.9$, $\mathrm{df}{=}5$, $p{=}0.011$,
$I^2{=}66\%$): two thirds of the between-model variation is real, not sampling error.
Four models fall between $1.22$ and $2.42$; both gemma models invert ($0.59$, $0.66$). A
DerSimonian--Laird random-effects estimate gives $1.33$, and its interval depends on what
one generalises over---treating the six models as exchangeable spans $1$
($[0.83, 2.14]$), while holding them fixed and resampling items as clusters gives
$[1.01, 1.73]$ ($P(\mathrm{OR}{\le}1){=}0.022$). We therefore claim the reallocation
\emph{across items} and not \emph{across models}: established for the six we ran, not for
models in general. Table~\ref{tab:permodel} shows that divergence descriptively
($-6.6$ to $+4.2$ overall); this is it tested.

\paragraph{Powering exposes disagreement, not only shrinkage.} At $n{\approx}100$ the
same test finds no significant heterogeneity ($Q{=}8.5$, $p{=}0.13$, $I^2{=}41\%$; the
native contrast lower still, $I^2{=}20\%$, $p{=}0.28$). The small-sample analysis did not
only overstate the effect---it made six models look as though they agreed. A placebo
rotation cannot catch that: it rotates the label and leaves the pooling assumption
untouched.

\paragraph{The reallocation begins before the optimizer.} Climbing the full prompting
ladder on the benchmark split (means over six models, \ona{}/mathematics): direct
answering $32.8/53.1$, CoT $33.3/74.6$, \bsfs{} $31.0/78.5$. Chain-of-thought alone buys
mathematics $+21.5$ and the native subject $+0.5$; the optimizer adds a further $+3.9$ to
mathematics and takes $-2.3$ from the native subject. The optimizer is not introducing
the reallocation so much as extending a gradient that CoT already established---which is
consistent with reasoning-subject demonstrations being the active ingredient, and with
the aggregate having been uninformative for both rungs.

\paragraph{The correction, and what produced it.} Our first pass used the benchmark's
own test split---$100$ \ona{} items per model, the scale at which subject-level audits
are normally run. There, \bsfs{} eroded \ona{} in five of six models ($75$ CoT-correct
answers flipping wrong against $46$ the other way, $p{=}0.011$), and the comparative
contrast gave an odds ratio of $2.27$ $[1.37, 3.91]$ against non-native items. We ran
the obvious placebo---rotate the ``native'' label through every subject and re-run the
identical estimator---and it passed: \tarix{}, the benchmark's other knowledge subject
and the closest structural analogue to a language-and-literature subject, returned
$1.04$ (Table~\ref{tab:placebo}, left).

On the frozen corpus that separation disappears. The native subject falls to $1.25$
$[0.98, 1.61]$ and \tarix{} rises to $1.16$ $[0.82, 1.64]$; the gap between them goes
from $1.23$ to $0.09$. The absolute native effect is null on every scoring (deployment
$p{=}0.283$; knowledge-scored $p{=}0.602$, direction reversed; rescue-parsed
$p{=}0.755$). Meanwhile the reasoning subjects remain below $1$ on both scales. The
signal was never native-versus-non-native; it was knowledge-versus-reasoning, plus
enough sampling noise at $n{\approx}100$ to make the native subject look singular.

We note that a subject-level audit is exactly where this failure mode is invisible.
Nothing about the $n{\approx}100$ analysis was cutting corners: it was paired, exact,
Holm-corrected, model-stratified, item-cluster-bootstrapped, and placebo-controlled. It
was simply too small, and the placebo was too small along with it.

Table~\ref{tab:allors} puts every odds ratio in the paper on one axis. The four
estimates above $1.6$---across two languages, two serving stacks and two
optimizers---all come from cells of at most $100$ native items per model. The single
estimate built on $393$ is $1.25$. We do not think this is a coincidence about which
settings happen to be harmful; we think it is what a small-sample contrast looks like
when it is measured repeatedly.

\paragraph{Per model, and what it costs to average.} Table~\ref{tab:permodel} breaks the
audit out by model. Native accuracy falls in four of six, which on its own would be a
weak claim. The informative column is the gap: qwen3.5:4b gains $4.2$ points overall
while losing $2.0$ on the native subject, and qwen3.6:27b gains $1.5$ overall while
losing $0.2$. A practitioner comparing aggregate scores across these models would rank
them in an order almost unrelated to what their Uzbek-speaking users experience.

\paragraph{One caveat we cannot fully separate.} The powered evaluation changes two
things at once: more items, and \emph{different} items (a 2019 public release rather
than the benchmark). Baseline accuracies are comparable across the two
(\ona{} $35.8$ vs.\ ${\sim}33$; \tarix{} $55.9$ vs.\ $50.7$), so we do not think corpus
shift explains the convergence, but the design cannot rule it out and we do not claim
otherwise.

\section{Which Demonstrations Do Harm}\label{sec:demos}

% it is reported as an observation, not a finding. See the paragraph on eight distinct
% demonstrations before strengthening any claim in this section.

If correctness-selection is the vehicle, what does it transmit? We ran the ablation that
separates the candidates (Table~\ref{tab:substitution}): four demonstrations drawn
uniformly from each model's own correct CoT traces, three seeds per cell, split by
whether they satisfy the prompt's brevity instruction, against \bsfs{}'s own picks.

\paragraph{Not compliance.} The constraint contrast is a clean null ($p{=}0.900$). The
repair we proposed---require the demonstration to satisfy the constraint it will
teach---does not work through the constraint.

\paragraph{Not subject mix, and not length.} The random non-compliant arm averages
$90\%$ reasoning-subject demonstrations and a $3{,}026$-character payload---more skewed
and more verbose than vanilla's $2{,}373$---and scores $32.0$, indistinguishable from the
random compliant arm at $50\%$ and $1{,}803$. This independently reconfirms two earlier
nulls of ours: a controlled experiment varying demonstration \emph{subject} mix
(four models, no contrast significant) and a $2{\times}2$ varying subject $\times$ length
(long-vs-short $b{=}82$, $c{=}79$, $p{=}0.875$). Neither what the demonstrations are
about nor how long they are determines the outcome.

\paragraph{An observation we cannot yet promote to a finding.} \bsfs{}'s own picks are the
\emph{worst} arm in Table~\ref{tab:substitution}, at $28.5$: below every random draw
($32.0$--$32.3$) and below using no demonstrations at all ($33.3$). Taken at face value
($b{=}105$, $c{=}147$, $p{=}0.0097$) that would say correctness-selection picks
worse-than-chance from among its own candidates---a claim about the selection rule.

It does not yet support that claim, for a reason we discovered only after running the
ablation. The training pool is shuffled with a fixed seed shared across models, and
\bsfs{} keeps the first $k$ examples that pass, so every model walks the same queue.
Across six models and $24$ demonstration slots the optimizer selected just \textbf{eight
distinct questions}; two models selected identical sets, and most pairs overlap in two to
three of four. The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:substitution} is therefore between
\emph{one} \bsfs{} draw, inherited by every model, and several independent random draws.
Its pooled $p$-value treats four models as independent evidence when the effective number
of \bsfs{} draws is close to one. We cannot distinguish ``the selection rule is bad''
from ``this particular draw was bad, and every model inherited it,'' and we do not claim
the former.

Resolving it needs a manipulation of the selection procedure rather than of the test set:
recompiling \bsfs{} under several pool orderings per model and comparing the resulting
distribution against the random-draw distribution. We have not run it, and we flag that
adding test items would \emph{not} substitute for it---more items estimate the same
single draw more precisely.

\paragraph{What the shared draw shows on its own.} That eight-of-twenty-four figure is
worth stating independently of the ablation. Six models spanning $4$B to $31$B parameters,
each nominally self-selecting demonstrations tailored to itself, converge on eight
questions---because selection is dominated by the ordering seed and by which items are
easy enough for every model to answer, not by the model. It is also why we now read the
apparent subject skew of \S\ref{sec:budget} as a property of one ordering rather than of
correctness-selection.

The constraint null is on firmer ground: both of its arms are random draws with three
seeds each, so it is not exposed in the same way. It remains a null on $1{,}200$ paired
observations---adequate to say the constraint does not help, not to bound how small a
true effect could be.

One route from optimizer to failure is identifiable and repairable. The demonstrations
\bsfs{} selects are the verbose ones---selected reasoning reaches $997$ characters per
demonstration and $2{,}373$ per compiled prompt---so the compiled prompt contradicts its
own brevity instruction, and under a fixed budget the inflated responses truncate.

We record a correction here, because an earlier version of this analysis attributed that
verbosity to subject skew. Measured against the wrong baseline, correctness-selection
appears to over-sample reasoning subjects $2.3\times$ ($71\%$ of selected demonstrations
against the benchmark's $30\%$). But the optimizer never sees the benchmark's subject
mix: the training pool is capped per subject and is $50\%$ reasoning by construction.
Against the pool it actually draws from, selection is $58\%$ reasoning across
demonstration slots and $50\%$ across \emph{distinct} selected questions---essentially
no subject over-selection at all. The verbosity of the selected demonstrations is real
and is what matters here; their subject composition is not the reason for it.

\paragraph{The budget is causal on the outcome.} Truncations fall
$28{\to}16{\to}3{\to}0$ on gemma4:e4b as \texttt{max\_tokens} rises $256{\to}2048$
(Table~\ref{tab:dose}), but that trend is a manipulation check---raising a cap
mechanically reduces cap-hits. The causal question is accuracy. Pairing bootstrap runs
item-by-item between the budget extremes, native accuracy improves in exactly the two
models that truncate ($b{=}11$, $c{=}25$, $n{=}200$, $p{=}0.029$) and not in the
zero-truncation control ($b{=}4$, $c{=}2$, $p{=}0.69$). The treated set is defined by
truncation counts, not by outcome.

\paragraph{And it is a minority of the effect.} Classifying every harmful flip on the
powered corpus, $46$ of $262$ ($17.6\%$) are truncation-attributable and none are format
drift; the remaining $216$ are well-formed, in-budget, correctly parsed answers that are
simply wrong. The route is real---gemma4:e4b's parse failures rise $72{\to}223$ under
vanilla bootstrapping---and it explains under a fifth of what happens.

address most of the effect.

\section{Two Regimes, and Two Non-Results}\label{sec:regimes}

\paragraph{Within-subject optimization is safe.} Bootstrapping demonstrations from
\ona{} training items only, evaluated across six models and $n{=}3{,}822$ paired items,
finds no erosion: the benchmark half shows a small improvement ($b{=}129$, $c{=}166$,
$p{=}0.036$ uncorrected, positive in $6/6$ models) and the replication half is exactly
null ($b{=}250$, $c{=}249$, $p{=}1.0$). The harm needs the cross-subject regime, where
the optimizer can import demonstrations from elsewhere. A single-model pilot that
suggested otherwise ($-7.2$, $p{=}0.045$, $n{=}250$) does not survive this.

\paragraph{Precision is not the explanation, and cells are unstable.} Serving the three
large models at bf16 rather than Q4 produces erosions of $-5$ on two of three, so the
effect is not a quantization artifact---but every model's delta flips sign between
precisions, because precision changes the correct-set and hence which demonstrations get
selected. These are $n{=}100$ single-seed cells and we draw only the negative conclusion
plus the actionable one: audits must run on the deployment configuration.

\paragraph{Tokenizer fertility, corrected.} We hypothesised that Uzbek prose is
token-expensive relative to math notation, making fixed budgets effectively smaller for
\ona{}~\citep[cf.][]{petrov2023tokenizer,lundin2025tokentax}. The logged counts falsify
it: math reasoning costs \emph{more} tokens than \ona{} reasoning, per character
(${\sim}0.43$ vs.\ ${\sim}0.38$) and per item (${\sim}130$--$160$ vs.\ ${\sim}93$--$124$).
The native subject is budget-fragile because its \emph{inputs} are longest (mean $337$
characters vs.\ $156$ for math), not because its outputs are dear.

\section{Generalization}\label{sec:travel}

\paragraph{A second language.} We ran the identical protocol on
\textsc{TurkishMMLU}~\citep{turkishmmlu2024} (Turkish Language and Literature against
Mathematics, Physics and History; $65$ test items per subject, six models). The
concealment reproduces in a starker form: aggregate accuracy rises $5.2$ points
($66.4{\to}71.6$) while the native subject gains nothing ($58.5{\to}58.2$)---Mathematics
$+9.7$, Physics $+7.2$, History $+4.1$. The absolute native effect is null
($b{=}37$, $c{=}36$, $p{=}1.0$).

Turkish also shows the native-specific pattern our Uzbek data no longer supports
(native $2.35$ $[1.38, 4.17]$ against History at $1.01$). We do not now believe that
separation without powering it: it is the same pattern, at $65$ items per subject, that
dissolved in Uzbek at $393$. Notably History gains $+4.1$ where Uzbek \tarix{} does not,
so the two benchmarks may genuinely differ in whether history behaves as a knowledge
subject. Powering \textsc{TurkishMMLU} is the obvious next experiment; until then we
report the Turkish differential as unconfirmed.

\paragraph{A second optimizer.} \textsc{MIPROv2}~\citep{opsahlong2024mipro}, which
searches instructions by Bayesian optimization rather than selecting demonstrations,
produced the same differential magnitude as \bsfs{} on the same items
($1.75$ $[0.99, 3.14]$ vs.\ $1.63$ $[0.97, 2.79]$; four treated models, both intervals
including $1$). Since the estimand itself attenuates under powering, we read this only as
evidence that the phenomenon is not a \bsfs{} implementation quirk.

Its mechanism evidence is sturdier and cuts the other way from payload accounts. On
gemma4:e4b, \textsc{MIPROv2}'s demonstrations are \emph{more} verbose than \bsfs{}'s
(mean $755$ vs.\ $631$ reasoning characters) yet produce $15$ truncated and $14$
unparseable generations against $62$ and $56$, and \ona{} \emph{rises} $3$ points. The
difference is the instruction it wrote, demanding ``a mandatory, very brief
justification'' concluding with ``\emph{only} the correct single-letter choice''---it
enforces the format its own demonstrations violate. Length alone does not determine the
format tax; the contradiction between demonstrations and instruction does.

\section{Discussion}

The stable result is that automatic prompt optimization is not a uniform improvement but
a \emph{reallocation}. It buys reasoning accuracy; it does not buy knowledge accuracy;
and on our benchmark those offset each other closely enough that the aggregate metric
reports nothing happened. For a national-language deployment this is the worst possible failure mode: the
number used to justify shipping is exactly the number that cannot see the cost.

\paragraph{On our own corrections.} Two claims in our first pass did not survive
contact with more data: that the harm was native-language-specific, and that a
brevity-constraint metric repaired it. Both were measured carefully at
$n{\approx}100$---paired, exact, corrected, placebo-controlled---and both dissolved when
powered. We think the generalisable lesson is not that we were careless but that
subject-level audits at that scale are underpowered for the contrasts they are used to
draw, and that the standard defences, placebo rotations included, are underpowered in
exactly the same proportion. A placebo at $n{\approx}100$ certified a separation that a
placebo at $n{\approx}400$ removed.

\paragraph{What we cannot explain.} \bsfs{}'s demonstrations underperform random draws
from the same correct pool and underperform no demonstrations at all. Subject mix, length
and compliance are each measured and null. But the optimizer's selection is also
near-degenerate under a fixed pool ordering---eight distinct demonstrations across six
models---so we cannot yet separate a bad selection \emph{rule} from a bad selection.
That separation is the experiment we would run next.

Four recommendations follow. (1)~\textbf{Audit per subject, not in aggregate}, and on
the deployment configuration. (2)~\textbf{Power the audit}: our $n{\approx}100$ analysis
was wrong in a specific, confident and reproducible way. (3)~\textbf{Randomise the pool ordering per model, and check what was selected}: with a
fixed seed the optimizer returned near-identical demonstrations for models spanning an
order of magnitude in size.
(4)~\textbf{Score parse failures separately} from knowledge errors.

\section*{Limitations}
\paragraph{Scale is uneven across sections.} The audit (\S\ref{sec:powered}) rests on
$5{,}058$ held-out paired items and is the paper's firmest result. The demonstration
ablation (\S\ref{sec:demos}) rests on $1{,}200$ paired observations and, for its
substitution contrast, on a single effective \bsfs{} draw; the Turkish and
\textsc{MIPROv2} arms rest on $n{\approx}65$ and $n{\approx}100$ per model. Given that we
have twice overturned our own $n{\approx}100$ findings, those sections should be read as
suggestive, and we say where each stands rather than presenting a uniform confidence.
\paragraph{Powering changes two variables.} The replication corpus is both larger and
different material. Baseline accuracies are comparable, but corpus shift is not fully
separable from sample size here.
\paragraph{Greedy decoding is not reproducible for every model.} Re-running the identical
CoT program in a second session at temperature $0$, all four qwen models reproduce
item-for-item ($251/251$); both gemma models do not, changing correctness on $12/100$ and
$7/100$ \ona{} items, for accuracy drifts of $-2.0$ and $+1.0$ points. That is the floor
beneath every per-model gemma cell, including the dose-response deltas and the precision
flips.
\paragraph{Scope.} Two benchmarks, two languages, two optimizers, inference-time
prompting only; reflective and reinforcement-style optimizers may differ. The
\textsc{MIPROv2} arm rests on four treated models, since the optimizer returned the
baseline unchanged for two under our search budget. Subject-type grouping
(knowledge/reasoning) is ours and is not the only defensible partition of four subjects.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
DTM derives from publicly available national university-entrance examination materials
and contains no personal data. Our aim is protective and self-correcting. We report that
a widely deployed optimization method reallocates accuracy in a way its headline metric
cannot express; that two of our own central claims required retraction in print after we
powered them; and that our own proposed repair does not work for the reason we proposed
it. All corrections appear alongside the claims they revise rather than in a footnote.
